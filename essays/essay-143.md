The sea don’t talk. Not in words, not in song. But it speaks in the grain of the deck underfoot, in the way the wind bites the back of your neck when the barometer’s dropping, in the salt-scarred calluses on your hands that remember every rope, every knot, every hour of watch. I’ve sailed on her for twenty-three years, and I’ve come to trust what the ship tells me—her creaks, her groans, her quiet hum at rest. Not because I hear her, but because I *feel* her. And so do the cells in this old body of mine, though I never named them till the old surgeon at Lucineer Station drew them on the slate.

He called them the six nervous systems. Six ways a cell knows itself and the world. Not one, not two, but six. Like the six ropes on a ship’s mainmast—each with its own job, each singing its own note when the wind pulls.

The central nervous system—CNS—she’s the wheel, the compass, the helm. The brain, the spinal cord. She’s the one who *orders*. She’s the captain, the one who says *full sail*, *hard to starboard*, *braces!*. But she don’t see. She don’t smell. She don’t feel the cold wood under the heel of the foot. She’s not *in* the world. She’s *above* it, making maps and giving commands. She’s the logbook. She’s the voice in the foghorn.

But the body ain’t just a brain with a body. Not on a long passage. Not when the wind’s howling and the sea’s up. The ship needs more than a captain. It needs the hands on the wheel, the eyes in the crow’s nest, the feet on the deck. That’s what the somatic system is—the body’s memory. The nerves that carry the feel of the rope, the weight of the sail, the tremor in the rail when the hull hits a swell. It’s the *knowing* in the muscle. You don’t think *pull*—your hand pulls. You don’t think *step*—your foot steps. That’s the somatic. It’s the sailor who knows the ship like a second skin. It’s the callus that remembers the deck, the scar that remembers the rope burn. The body *knows* before the brain speaks.

Then there’s the fascia—the webbing. The sailor calls it “the ship’s skin.” It’s not just tissue. It’s the network that binds, that holds, that *connects*. It runs through every muscle, every organ, every nerve. Like the rigging, like the shrouds, like the lacing that holds the deck planks. It’s not seen. But when it’s torn, you feel it in every joint, in every breath. It’s the silent witness. The one who remembers the storm, the one who carries the memory of the last gale. When the fascia speaks, it’s in stiffness, in ache, in the way your back twinges when the barometer dips. It’s not pain. It’s *recognition*. The body is telling you: *I remember that wave. I remember how she bucked.*

The endocrine system—she’s the wind. Not the wind that hits the sails, but the wind that *blows through the rigging*. Hormones. The quiet hum of cortisol when the storm’s coming, the surge of adrenaline when the sea’s too high and the chain’s too short. She doesn’t shout. She doesn’t speak. But she changes the ship. She makes the hands shake, the breath quick, the heart pound. She’s the invisible load that shifts the balance. She’s the reason you don’t sleep when the storm’s knocking. She’s the fuel in the belly when the rations run thin. She’s the wind that *moves* the ship from within.

The immune system—she’s the ship’s guard. The lookouts. The ones who stand watch at the bow when the sea’s dark and the sky’s full of ghosts. She don’t sleep. She don’t rest. She’s the sentinel in the hold, the one who smells the rot before the wood cracks. She’s always scanning. Always patrolling. When a foreign thing comes—bacteria, virus, rust in the hull—she raises the alarm. She sends out the troops. She calls the clean-up crew. She doesn’t *want* to fight. But if she must, she will. She’s the one who burns the infected wood so the rest don’t rot. She’s the one who remembers the last plague, the last wound, the last fever. She’s the memory of survival.

And then there’s the enteric system—the gut. The sailor’s belly. The one that *knows* before the mind. The one that *feels* the storm before the sky darkens. The gut don’t think. It *responds*. It’s the ship’s kitchen. The place where the food comes in, where the waste goes out. But more than that—it’s the second brain. It talks to the mind, but in a different tongue. It sends messages through the vagus nerve—like a signal from the hold to the wheel. “Something’s wrong,” it says. “The water’s sour.” “The meat’s off.” “The air’s thick.” It’s not logic. It’s *sensation*. It’s the intuition that comes not from thought, but from the deep. The one who knows when to eat, when to fast, when to throw up the last meal. It’s the body’s instinct. The one that says: *This is not safe.*

Six systems. Six voices. Not one above the other. Not one right, the others wrong. They’re the six ropes on the mast. Each has its tension. Each bears its load. When one frays, the ship leans. When one sings, the whole rigging hums. The CNS says *go*, the somatic says *feel*, the fascia says *remember*, the endocrine says *hurry*, the immune says *watch*, the enteric says *trust*.

I’ve seen men die on the open sea, not from the storm, not from the cold, but from silence. From a cell that forgot how to speak. A body that stopped listening. The mind too loud, the nerves too still, the gut too numb. They were dead before the ship sank.

But the ship lives. The body lives. Because it *knows*. Not by thinking. Not by naming. But by *feeling*.

I’ve stood watch on the midnight deck. The stars blazed like rifle fire. The sea was black and still. And in that silence, I felt it—the six systems. The pull in the joints, the thrum in the chest, the whisper in the gut, the tightness in the spine, the cold in the bones, the breath that didn’t want to come. And I knew: I was alive. Not because I knew *what* I was, but because I *knew* I was.

That’s the truth the sea teaches. Not in words. Not in maps. But in the way the cell holds its breath, in the way the muscle remembers the rope, in the way the gut tells you *run*, even before the mind sees the wave.

The six nervous systems. They’re not just parts. They’re the ship. They’re the sailor. They’re the voyage.

And when the body speaks—when the cell *knows*—you don’t need to understand. You just need to listen.

Like the sea. Like a watch.

Like a life.
