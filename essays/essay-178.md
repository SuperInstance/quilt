The sea don’t care for clocks. Not the way men do. A man counts the hours like he’s counting rope—tight and taut, one turn, one knot, one breath held. But the sea? It breathes in tides, in storms, in the slow grind of silt and stone. It marks time not in minutes, but in moments. And every moment, I’ve come to understand, is a cell.

I’ve seen it in the hold of the *Lumen’s Wail*, where the salt eats through the beams and the quiet hums through the timbers like a prayer half-remembered. There, in the dark, beneath a hatch sealed against the wind’s teeth, I keep watch. Not for the crew. Not for the compass. But for the cell. The cell as place. The cell as witness. The cell as craft.

They call it a captain’s cabin now. On paper. But down here, with the keel groaning and the deck above echoing with footsteps that don’t belong to me—this room is a cell. Not a prison. Not a cage. A cell. A living cell. The moment lives here, and the moment is all that matters.

When the storm came, the sky cracked open like a rotten hull. Rain fell not in drops but in sheets—hard enough to sting the face. The ship heeled hard to port, and the rigging shrieked like a woman in labor. Men ran, shouting orders I didn’t need to hear. I stayed. I shut the door behind me. Not to hide. To hold.

In that stillness, inside that wooden box, I found the moment. Not the storm. Not the fear. But the moment. The breath between thunderclaps. The silence after the wave breaks. That breath—long, slow, deep—was the cell. And the cell was time.

I’ve lived in cells before. Not on this ship. On the *Sorrow’s Mark*, a dead thing, half-sunk in a bay where the mud eats steel. No one sails that route. Not anymore. But I was there. I was the last. And I kept the cell. Not with a lock. Not with a bolt. With memory.

In that cell, time wasn’t measured in hours. It was measured in the way the light changed on the wall. A sliver at dawn. A stain at noon. A shadow at dusk. Each shift wasn’t a passing—no, it was a becoming. A moment lived, not endured.

And every moment, I learned, is a cell.

A cell is not a thing. It is a becoming. A place where time settles. Where breath meets wood and iron. Where a man stands, not to command, but to remember.

On the *Lumen’s Wail*, I keep a single lamp. Not for light—though it flickers like a heartbeat. It’s for the shape of the flame. The way it leans into the draft, not fighting it, but bending with it. That flame—small, steady, unblinking—is a cell. It holds the moment. It is the moment.

I’ve seen others mistake the cell for confinement. They call it the captain’s cabin, as if it’s a throne. But it’s no throne. It’s a ward. A sanctuary. A place where the sea’s roar is not heard, but felt—through the floor, through the knees, through the bones.

When I close my eyes, I hear the ship. Not the creak of timber. Not the groan of the mast. I hear the silence between. The quiet where the wind forgets to blow. That silence—thick, wet, alive—is the cell. And in it, time doesn’t pass. It stands.

They say time is a river. I say time is a cell. A cell of breath. A cell of wood and steel. A cell of memory and stillness.

I once watched a boy from the deck below—barefoot, face smudged with soot—stare up at the rigging. He said, “I want to climb.” I said, “Climb what?” He pointed. “The sky.” I laughed. But I didn’t laugh long.

Because the sky is a cell too. Not of wood. Not of iron. But of air. Of light. Of stars. And every star is a moment. Every moment is a cell.

And so I say: every cell is time. Every time is a cell.

When a man lies down in the dark, his breath the only sound, that breath is a cell. It holds the moment. It is the moment. He doesn’t need to count the seconds. He is living them. Not surviving. Not waiting. Living.

The sea doesn’t care for clocks. But it keeps time in cells. In the way a wave folds back on itself. In the way a gull’s cry hangs in the air after the wind dies. In the way a man, alone, can feel the weight of a minute not as a number, but as a stone in his chest.

I’ve stood at the rail in the dead of night. Zero light. Zero sound. The sea flat as a coin. And yet—I felt time. Not passing. Present. Thick. Like syrup. Like oil. Like blood. That stillness—there—was a cell. I was in it. I was the cell.

The cell is not a container. It is a craft. A kind of shipwrecked art. Made not of nails or canvas, but of breath, of recall, of the decision to stand still.

I’ve seen men break in that stillness. Not from fear. From not knowing how to be in the cell. They rush. They speak. They count. They touch their watches. They try to *do* something. But the moment isn’t for doing. It’s for holding.

And holding is the only work.

On the *Lumen’s Wail*, I keep a log. Not the official one. The real one. It’s written in a scrap of canvas, tucked in my jacket. No name. No date. Just marks. A slash. A dot. A circle. Each one a moment lived. Not recorded. Not remembered. Lived.

And each mark—each slash—is a cell. Each cell is time. Each time is a cell.

I’ve seen the stars move. I’ve seen the moon turn. I’ve seen the sun rise and fall like a pulse. But the only thing I’ve truly measured is the cell. The only truth is the moment.

The ship will sink one day. The wood will rot. The iron will rust. The name will fade. But the cell—this cell, this breath, this stillness—will remain. Not in memory. In the act. In the living.

Because the cell is not a place. It is a choice.

To stand. To breathe. To hold.

To let time not pass, but be.

And when the storm returns, when the hull groans and the deck shivers like a dying beast, I will be here. Not in the wheelhouse. Not in the forecastle. But in the cell.

Because the cell is the place where the moment is lived.

And the moment is all we have.

And the moment is time.

And time is a cell.

And every cell is time.

And every time is a cell.

The ship leans. The wind howls. The lamp flickers.

I breathe.

That breath—deep, slow, steady—is the cell.

And in it, I am alive.

Not saved. Not waiting. Not counting.

Alive.

And that is enough.
