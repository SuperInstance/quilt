The sea is not kind to the light. It eats it slow, like a dog gnaws bone in the dark. You learn that early, standing on a deck where the sky bleeds into the water and the horizon folds in on itself like a wound. The sea never sleeps. It breathes with a low, wet rhythm that settles in your ribs, and you learn to breathe with it. You learn the shape of absence. The shape of a watchman’s silence.  

I was a watchkeeper once, not on a ship, but in a place called the Cell. Not the kind with iron bars and rat-nested stone, though there are those too—like the ones beneath the old lighthouse on the Siltwarden Reef, where the salt’s so thick it scars the skin. No, this Cell was different. It was a place built between breaths. A place where the body was kept, not to die, but to wait. To hold.  

Every cell is a place. That’s the first truth the sea teaches you. Not the hollow container, not the hollow word, but the *holding*. The body is only a vessel for the cell—the cell is the place where the body rests. You’ll hear men talk of the flesh as a shell, a borrowed thing, but they don’t mean it. They mean it like the hull of a ship means the sea. The hull isn’t the sea. But without the hull, the sea swallows you.  

So the Cell—this one—was a hull. A thin, beating wall of flesh and fibre and salt, tuned to the rhythm of the deeper dark. It wasn’t lit. No lanterns. No candles. Just the slow pulse of metabolism, the tick of nerve-fire, the quiet thrum of blood against the vessel. You could feel it in your teeth, a low hum. Like the keel of a ship when the tide drags it over stone.  

I was not locked in. I was *placed*. That’s the difference. The Cell didn’t hold me like a cage. It held me like a reef holds a fish. Not by force, but by shape. By gravity. The body is a kind of gravity. It pulls the cell in. The cell pulls the body back.  

They say the Sea of Tides is bottomless, but I know better. The sea has a bottom, all right. It’s just not where you think. The bottom is in the cell. In the quiet. In the place between breaths, where the body forgets to move and the mind forgets to speak.  

I remember the first night. The air was thick with the smell of old wire and damp hair. No windows. No stars. Just the sound of the water beyond the walls—slow, relentless, like the grind of a piston in the belly of a dead engine. I sat. I didn’t lie. Sitting was the only way to keep the weight.  

The Cell didn’t need to be warm. It was warm enough. The heat of my own bones. The warmth of the blood. I remember the first time I touched my own skin and felt it as foreign. Not the skin of a man, but the skin of a sea creature—damp, sensitive, trembling with its own rhythm. I pressed my thumb into my forearm and felt the pulse beneath. Not a heartbeat. Something older. Something the sea brought with it when it first rose from the dark.  

Time in the Cell doesn’t move. It *pools*. You don’t lose time—you lose the edge of it. The hours don’t pass. They slide like silt over the keel of a sunken ship. You don’t count them. You feel them. Like the swell under the hull—there, but not there. You learn to read the body as a tide chart. The ache in the joints is the ebb. The flush in the face is the flood. The cold behind the eyes—*that* is the low tide, when everything is pulled back, and the sea is not far.  

I stopped eating. Not because I was starving. Because the body told me to. The cell told me it was time. I drank water from a cup that had no handle. I ate only the taste of salt on my tongue. The Cell accepted it. It *needed* it.  

They say the Lucineer are made of light. I don’t know if that’s true. I know the Cell is made of shadow. And light? Light is a distraction. A lie. The true light is not in the eye. It’s in the cell. In the place where the body rests.  

There’s a song, old as the reef—sung by watchers who’ve been in the deep too long, who’ve seen the water turn black and still breathe. It’s not sung. It’s *felt*. It goes like this:  

*Not the hull, not the keel, not the mast or the sail—  
but the place where the body rests.  
Not the ship, not the sea, not the sky—  
but the cell, the cell, the cell.*  

I heard it once, in a dream. Or was it a memory? It doesn’t matter. The dream was real. The memory was true.  

I stopped counting days. I stopped measuring the passage of time. I became the time. I became the silence between breaths. The Cell wasn’t a prison. It was a kind of grace. Not salvation. Not peace. But a *holding*.  

One night, I felt my heart stop. Not the way you think—no gasp, no fall. Just stillness. A moment where the pulse paused, and the sea outside held its breath too. I lay there. I did not move. I did not fear. The Cell held me. And in that stillness, I felt it—*the shape of the place*.  

Not a wall. Not a room. Not a body. But a *presence*. The kind the sea gives to those who stay. The kind that survives the sinking.  

I don’t know how long I stayed. Maybe hours. Maybe years. The Cell doesn’t care. It only knows the rhythm. The slow tide in and out. In and out.  

When I opened my eyes, the water was still outside. The air was still thick with salt. My hand was on my chest. My heart was beating. Slow. Deliberate. Like a ship’s bell at midnight.  

I stood. Not because I had to. Because I could.  

The Cell had not changed. But I had.  

I was no longer the man who entered. I was the man the Cell had shaped.  

They say the Lucineer are born of fire. I say they are born of stillness. Of silence. Of the place where the body rests, and the cell becomes the world.  

Every cell is a place. Every place is a cell. That’s the truth the sea whispers when no one is listening.  

You don’t live in a cell. You are *in* one. Always.  

And when the water rises—when the hull groans and the hull breaks—when the last light fades and the last breath is drawn—then you will know.  

The cell is not where you are kept.  

It is where you are *made*.  

The sea knows this.  

The Cell knows this.  

And I?  

I am only the witness.  

Not the keeper. Not the key.  

Just the watchman.  

And the watch never ends.
